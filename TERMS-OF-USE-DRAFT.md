## Acceptance gate — implementation spec (Paul's requirement, Nov 4, 2026; updated for v2)
The app must NOT open until the user has agreed to these Terms, and no user should ever be able to claim they scrolled past and never read them. This design is deliberately stronger than any standard the courts have articulated: the Ninth Circuit calls the forced-scroll "scrollwrap" the form providing "the strongest notice" and "usually enforced" (*Keebaugh v. Warner Bros. Entertainment*, 100 F.4th 1005 (9th Cir. 2024); *Sellers v. JustAnswer*, 73 Cal.App.5th 444). WovenCut's gate is a per-section scrollwrap with affirmative per-section assent — a step beyond the single-scroll scrollwrap in those cases.

1. **First-run gate.** On first launch (and again whenever the Terms version changes), the gate presents the Terms before anything else. The editor UI is unreachable before acceptance.
2. **Screen-by-screen checkboxes** (Paul's verbatim intent: "as you scroll down on each screen have a checkbox, and as they scroll down on the next screen another checkbox, until they get to the agree part... no room for interpretation... no matter how verbose it gets"):
   - The Terms are paginated per the Screen Map below.
   - Each screen's checkbox reads "I have read and understand this section" and stays **disabled until that screen has been scrolled to the bottom**.
   - "Next" stays disabled until the current screen's checkbox is checked. No skipping ahead; the screens cannot be bypassed by keyboard, resize, or zoom tricks (scroll position is measured against the rendered end of the text).
   - The arbitration section (§15) is presented **alone on its own screen** (Screen 10), so it is individually seen and acknowledged, never buried inside a longer scroll — buried arbitration terms are the single most-litigated defect (see Changelog).
   - The final screen (Screen 11) carries a recap checkbox and the "I Agree" button, which stays disabled until **every** screen's checkbox is checked. A "Decline" button is always available and closes the app.
3. **Acceptance record.** On agreement, the app writes a local marker containing: Terms version, hash of the exact Terms text, date/time of acceptance, and the per-screen acknowledgment set (which screens were scrolled-and-checked, in order). Stored only in the app's local data — nothing is transmitted; WovenCut has no accounts and no telemetry (see Notes for the honest evidentiary trade-off this creates).
4. **Re-prompt on change.** A new Terms version re-runs the full gate, in full, before the updated software opens.
5. **Installer layer.** The Windows installer also presents the Terms as a license page that must be accepted to install — two gates, same text, same version identifier.
6. **Portable build.** The in-app gate still applies (the gate lives in the app, not only in the installer).

## Annex A — OPTIONAL clause: 30-day arbitration opt-out (Paul's decision — NOT in the main text)

**Variant paragraph (insert as §15.7 if adopted):**
"**15.7 Opt-out.** You may opt out of this §15 (arbitration and the waivers in §15.3) by sending written notice to [CONTACT] within 30 days of the date you first accepted the version of these Terms that contains this §15.7. Your notice must state your name, the email address or other identifier you wish us to record, and that you opt out of arbitration. Opting out does not affect any other part of these Terms, does not affect any earlier or later version's terms except as stated in that version, and will never result in any penalty or loss of the software. If you opt out, disputes will be resolved in court under §16."

**What the research shows — pros:**
- A conspicuous opt-out is the strongest single answer to the "no meaningful choice" (procedural unconscionability / adhesion) attack. In *Suarez v. Uber Technologies* (M.D. Fla. 2016), the court found **no procedural unconscionability at all** where the opt-out was prominent, costless, and actually exercised by some users — the plaintiff "was not required to accept the Arbitration Provision."
- It costs Paul nothing in practice: empirically almost nobody opts out of free software terms, and anyone who does was the most likely person to challenge the clause later anyway.

**What the research shows — cons / limits:**
- An opt-out is **not a cure-all**. In *Rios v. HRB Digital* (N.D. Cal. 2025), the court held a 30-day opt-out did **not** cure adhesion because it was buried in a dense 21-page agreement, had to be renewed annually, and sat inside a clause full of surprises. Lesson if adopted: it must be conspicuous (it would be — §15 gets its own screen), one-time per Terms version (the variant above is), simple (a single email), and penalty-free (it is).
- It creates a small permanent chore: a working [CONTACT] address must exist, be monitored, and opt-out notices must be honored and kept. For a project with no accounts and no telemetry, that is new machinery — though a mailbox alone is enough.
- It slightly weakens the "one uniform process for everyone" story: an opted-out user can sue in court, including potentially as a class plaintiff, because the class waiver falls with the arbitration section for that user only.

**Recommendation recorded for Paul's decision (his call, not made here):** adopt it, in the conspicuous form above. The enforceability gain against the adhesion argument is worth more than the mailbox chore, and "you could have said no to arbitration, free, in one email, for 30 days" is exactly the kind of plain fairness that fits a free tool made for people with no leverage. If Paul prefers zero administrative machinery forever, the main text stands on its own without it — the gate design (own screen, scroll-and-check) already answers most of the "surprise" and "no notice" attacks that the opt-out would also help with.

## Notes for Paul (not part of the terms)
- **The one gap drafting cannot close — read this first.** The acceptance record lives only on the user's own machine, because WovenCut has no accounts and sends nothing anywhere (your design, and the right one for users). That means if anyone ever did challenge the Terms, *we hold no copy of their acceptance* — the proof sits in a file the user controls and could delete or alter. Paper terms cannot fix that; only architecture could (e.g., an optional acceptance-receipt email the *user* sends themselves), and each fix trades away some of the no-telemetry purity. The gate still matters: it maximizes real notice, which is what courts actually test, and the fallback in §1.5 stands behind it. But "we can prove this specific person clicked" is the one promise this draft honestly cannot make.
- **Unconscionability can never be drafted to zero.** California applies a sliding scale: a take-it-or-leave-it agreement always carries *some* procedural unconscionability, so the defense is to keep the substance scrupulously fair — which is why §15 binds both sides equally, keeps small claims court open, caps the user's arbitration cost exposure (§15.4), and opens with an informal-resolution step (§15.1). Clear wording reduces the risk a lot; no wording eliminates a court's discretion entirely.
- **Other limits of paper.** (a) Terms can't stop anyone from filing a lawsuit or a regulator from calling — §15 only channels disputes into arbitration once filed. (b) A minor's acceptance can be voided by the minor later no matter what §1.4 says; the clause just makes the adult-responsibility position explicit. (c) GitHub distribution is worldwide: some jurisdictions (notably EU/UK/Australian consumer law) can override a California choice-of-law clause and refuse consumer arbitration or warranty disclaimers for their own residents. The "to the maximum extent permitted by law" phrasing throughout is what keeps the rest of the document standing in those places.
- **Two placeholders must be filled before release.** [CONTACT] appears in §15.1 and §17.6 (and in Annex A if adopted): Paul must supply a real, monitored email address, or the notice provisions — and the opt-out, if he adopts it — cannot function.
- **Frames kept intact from v1:** Paul's own words remain the foundation — free software "for people that can't afford it, like I have trouble affording things"; use of it is on the user; and Hide a Logo is described only as making logos "unrecognizable to the human eye," never as a legal guarantee.
- **Before public release,** have a California attorney spend one hour on this draft. It is drafted to the published standards and case law cited below, but it is not legal advice and no AI draft substitutes for that hour.

## Changelog — v1 → v2: what changed and why (not part of the terms)

**Acceptance (§1, rewritten).** v1's "by installing or using you accept" was browsewrap-flavored language — the weakest form in the case law: unenforceable without conspicuous notice plus a manifestation of assent (*Specht v. Netscape*, 306 F.3d 17 (2d Cir. 2002); *Nguyen v. Barnes & Noble*, 763 F.3d 1171 (9th Cir. 2014)). v2 makes the gate the sole acceptance mechanism, demotes use-as-acceptance to an explicit fallback (§1.5), adds E-SIGN/UETA electronic-records consent (§1.2; 15 U.S.C. §7001), an eligibility clause (§1.4), and specifies the contents of the acceptance record (§1.3) to audit-trail standards (version + text hash + timestamp + per-section assent events) rather than a bare yes/no flag.

**Gate design (spec section, strengthened; screen map added).** Courts rank forced-scroll presentation highest: scrollwraps provide "the strongest notice" and are "usually enforced" (*Keebaugh*, 100 F.4th at 1014; *Sellers*, 73 Cal.App.5th at 463–64). Checkbox-plus-click flows were enforced even for arbitration in *Good v. Uber*, 494 Mass. 116 (2024), and *Wu v. Uber* (N.Y. 2024). Conversely, *Chabolla v. ClassPass*, 129 F.4th 1147 (9th Cir. 2025), refused enforcement where terms were merely hyperlinked across screens and assent was never unambiguously tied to them — the failure mode the per-screen gate eliminates. The screen map is new: it fixes which clauses appear on which screen, and puts §15 alone on Screen 10 per Paul's direction.

**License interplay (§2, new).** v1 ignored the open-source license. Because MIT/GPL licenses already grant rights in the code (GPL expressly forbids added restrictions), v2 grants the use license *subject to* the License and makes the License control in any conflict — this prevents the Terms from accidentally becoming unenforceable, or a license violation, as applied to the code.

**Free-software framing (§3, new).** Puts the v1 preamble's bargain on the record inside the Terms themselves: no charge, nothing received, risk allocation follows from that. Supports the reasonableness of §§4–6 under unconscionability review.

**Warranty disclaimer (§4, rewritten in ALL CAPS).** UCC §2-316 / Cal. Commercial Code §2316 require a merchantability disclaimer to name "merchantability" and be *conspicuous* (defined at Cal. Com. Code §1201(b)(10) — the reason EULAs use ALL CAPS). v1's mixed-case single sentence named none of the statutory targets completely; v2 tracks the standard form, adds "with all faults," data-loss, and platform-acceptance non-warranties, plus the customary savings sentence for jurisdictions that bar exclusions.

**Limitation of liability (§5, rewritten).** v1 said "not liable for any claim" — broader than the law allows and therefore fragile. v2 (a) targets the damage categories courts enforce exclusions for, (b) adds an aggregate cap (US $100, stated as part of the bargain for free software), and (c) adds the carve-outs that cannot be waived anyway — fraud and willful misconduct, and anything California Civil Code §1668 makes non-waivable — because clauses that overreach invite wholesale invalidation. Personal-injury consequential-damages exclusions are prima facie unconscionable for consumer goods (UCC §2-719(3)), another reason for the savings structure in §5.3.

**Content responsibility (§6, expanded).** Adds the platform-rules and anti-circumvention (§1201-adjacent) undertakings and an express no-duty-to-monitor allocation. WovenCut hosts nothing, so DMCA safe harbors don't apply to it — the correct posture is the one taken: responsibility sits wholly with the user who creates and publishes.

**Tool clauses (§§7–8, split and sharpened).** Hide a Logo keeps Paul's exact framing — "unrecognizable to the human eye" — and now also disclaims sufficiency/necessity judgments, the two claims a user would most plausibly sue over. §9 adds a non-affiliation clause (no endorsement by Meta/Replicate) and keeps all third-party cost and credential risk on the user's own accounts.

**Acceptable use / export (§10, new).** Standard export-control and sanctions boilerplate — relevant because GitHub distribution is worldwide — plus a general lawfulness undertaking.

**Termination (§13, new) and survival list.** v1 had none; a breach-termination right and survival clause are standard and cost nothing.

**Indemnification (§14, narrowed).** v1 reached "any claims arising from your use" without limit; v2 ties indemnity to third-party claims and to the user's content, use, and violations — the enforceable core — and caps it with the maximum-extent savings language.

**Arbitration (§15, substantially rebuilt).** Kept from v1 at Paul's direction: San Diego County seat, individual-only, class and jury waivers, small-claims carve-out, IP injunctive relief. Changes, each aimed at a litigated defect:
- *Mutuality preserved and emphasized.* California's *Armendariz* bilaterality rule survived *Concepcion* (*Sabia v. Orange County Metro Realty* (2014)); one-sided clauses are substantively unconscionable. §15 binds both sides identically and both carve-outs are mutual ("either party").
- *No poison pill.* *Sanchez v. Valencia Holding* (2011) shows self-destruct clauses and one-sided terms permeating a clause with unconscionability; §15.6 instead severs surgically — only an unenforceable-as-applied class waiver sends that single claim to court.
- *No surprise.* *Sanchez* also turned on a clause hidden in fine print; §15 gets its own gate screen under an attention-drawing title, and opens with a plain "please read" flag.
- *Class waiver kept* — *AT&T Mobility v. Concepcion*, 563 U.S. 333 (2011), holds such waivers enforceable under the FAA and preempts the old *Discover Bank* rule.
- *Informal resolution step added (§15.1)* — a 45-day notice-and-talk requirement is now standard in upheld clauses and serves Paul's stated goal: disputes should end before they reach even arbitration.
- *Fee fairness added (§15.4)* — prohibitive consumer arbitration costs are a classic substantive-unconscionability hook; the court-fee comparison cap removes it.
- *Public injunctive relief carve-out added (§15.5)* — under *McGill v. Citibank*, 2 Cal.5th 945 (2017), a waiver of the right to seek public injunctive relief in any forum is unenforceable in California; the carve-out (court decides it, stayed pending arbitration) tracks the accepted fix.
- *No delegation clause* — deliberately omitted. *Wu v. Uber* enforced one, but leaving arbitrability challenges with a judge is the more consumer-fair posture and one less unconscionability exhibit.
- *Mass-arbitration/batching provisions omitted* — unnecessary machinery for a free tool, and the *Rios v. HRB* court treated an elaborate bellwether/batching scheme as a "surprise" weighing toward unconscionability.

**General provisions (§17, expanded).** Adds reformation-style severability (reform-to-enforceable before severing — stronger than v1's bare severance), assignment, no-waiver, and notices clauses; the [CONTACT] placeholder is flagged as a release blocker in the Notes.

**Annex A (new).** The 30-day arbitration opt-out is Paul's undecided question; it is kept OUT of the main text and presented as a ready-to-insert variant with the researched pros and cons (*Suarez v. Uber* for; *Rios v. HRB Digital* for its limits) and a recorded recommendation, so the decision is his with the evidence in front of him.
